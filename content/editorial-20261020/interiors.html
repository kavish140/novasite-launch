An interior design enquiry often begins with a photograph and a vague request to make a home look similar. The studio still has to establish the property type, scope, constraints and whether the visitor wants design advice or execution. A useful website helps that conversation begin with more context. It should show how a space was planned, not simply collect a wall of attractive finishes.

For a Mumbai studio, the website can complement social discovery by organising projects into a reference that a household can review together. It need not imitate the pace or layout of a social feed. The implementation below is a proposed workflow, not a report of results from a SiteNova interiors client.

Start with the reader's room, scope and decision

A visitor comparing a kitchen renovation with a whole-flat redesign needs to recognise the relevant work quickly. Group projects by meaningful scope and property type, and allow a project to explain several rooms without creating nearly identical pages for every photograph. A stable project URL gives someone a dependable reference to share with a family member or another decision maker.

Describe what the studio was responsible for. Design consultation, procurement support and complete execution imply different responsibilities. Credit collaborators and explain which images show completed work rather than a concept. A project page should not imply that a rendering was photographed in a finished home or that a decorative item was custom-designed when it came from another supplier.

Write the process around approvals rather than slogans

The process page can explain when measurements are taken, how a brief becomes a design, how changes are reviewed and which decisions the customer must approve. Avoid presenting one universal schedule for every renovation. An occupied home, a building's work-hour restrictions and material availability can alter the sequence. Explain those dependencies without inventing a standard completion promise.

Use a sample approval journey where actual client material is confidential. Label it as a worked example and identify what the customer would review at each stage. A visitor should understand how to give feedback, who records the agreed revision and when a change can affect scope. The website does not need to expose the firm's internal project-management system to explain this clearly.

Define a maintainable project library

Ask the studio to nominate who will update the portfolio after a project closes. Store captions, scope, credits, permissions and alt text as part of the publishing record rather than relying on someone to remember them when uploading a folder. A project can remain unpublished until the required information is ready. That is preferable to filling the gaps with generic location copy or invented customer reactions.

Test maintenance using a real set of photographs, including a portrait image and a long room title. Staff should be able to withdraw an image and correct a credit without breaking the gallery. If a project URL changes, retain an appropriate redirect so existing shared links remain useful. This makes the portfolio an ongoing record of work rather than an opening-day design exercise.
